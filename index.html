The gallery

Four artifacts. Four truths.

Walk slowly. Each artifact carries a piece of this history — select it to uncover what it holds.

Station 01

The Corroded Figure

Corroded steel, flaking paint, broken glass

“The residential school system’s main goal was assimilation. Constant physical and psychological punishment reinforced the stripping of identity, leading to a forgotten culture, tradition, spirituality, language, and family segregation.”

Station 02

The Empty Tobacco Bowl

Carved stone, weathered cedar

“The lasting generational effects manifested as vague voids in communities—often resulting in struggles, addictions, and a constant, directionless search for meaning. Intergenerationally, children were stripped of the opportunity to learn their native traditions and language due to the forced assimilation of their parents.”

Station 03

Omitted Histories

Oil, textbook, posters, documents

“History classes have long hidden or sanitized past Indigenous events. True reconciliation efforts—such as establishing Indigenous councils and financial settlements—arose only after immense advocacy, proving that learning injustices as absolute truth is vital to public awareness.”

Station 04

The Blank White Paper

Paper, light

“Healing and reconciliation serve as a crucial public acknowledgment of previous injustices—an attempt to fill the void. While it cannot instantly fix the past, it provides warmth and allows unheard voices to finally speak.”

The gallery exit · a personal reflection

Dignity, Solidarity, and Faith

Over the course of this month, tremendous emotions were felt. Spikes of realization forced a deep questioning of personal beliefs: Was human dignity violated in Catholicism? If historical atrocities are simply dismissed as an “error,” why are Indigenous communities still treated differently today—struggling against ongoing ignorance and the violation of basic water rights?

If only they had been treated as true equals, culture would have been appreciated, assimilation prevented, and voices honored. Instead, reality often reveals a system where money acts as Canada’s idol.

To truly treat others as family means respecting their fundamental dignity and living in solidarity. Everyone holds a right and a responsibility to respect one another. Yet, residential schools violated this sacred trust—beating children unethically, torturing them in isolation, and driving many to tragedy.

This injustice must never go unrecognized. As Catholics and as global citizens today, our calling is clear: to stand up, acknowledge the truth, and actively protest for the enduring recognition and protection of Indigenous rights.

This closing reflection is the author’s own journal — offered in a spirit of solidarity, not as a substitute for Indigenous voices or spiritual traditions.
